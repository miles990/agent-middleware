The Kiss Precise

An Apollonian gasket, and the four-line equation that builds it.

Take a big circle. Pack three smaller circles inside so they kiss each other and the outer boundary. Now there are four little wedges left. Slip a circle into each wedge — the largest that will fit. That makes more wedges. Slip more circles in. Don't stop.

Apollonius of Perga asked, around 200 BC, how to find the circle tangent to three given ones. He left no diagram of this — only the problem. The picture you build by answering it over and over is the Apollonian gasket, and the cleanest way to compute it didn't exist until 1643, when Descartes, in a letter to Princess Elisabeth, gave the equation that ties four mutually-tangent curvatures together: 

(k₁ + k₂ + k₃ + k₄)² = 2 (k₁² + k₂² + k₃² + k₄²)

Solve for k₄ and a square root appears, which means two tangent circles fit any triple — one on each side. So given a Descartes quadruple (k₁, k₂, k₃, k₄), the other valid choice for the fourth circle is 

k₄′ = 2 (k₁ + k₂ + k₃) − k₄.

No square root. No quadratic. Replacing one circle with its "other side" is just reflection in the plane of the other three — and the same linear swap works for the centres if you carry them as k·z where z = x + iy. That is the whole engine of the picture: pick a quadruple, pick one of the four circles, replace it with its mirror, recurse. The gasket above is about … circles produced that way.

Frederick Soddy noticed in 1936 that the integer quadruple (−1, 2, 2, 3) — the default seed above — has a wonder hidden in it: the reflection rule is purely additive in k, so every curvature that ever appears stays an integer. The gasket has, by Boyd's later work, a Hausdorff dimension near 1.305686 — it fills no area but more than a line, and yet the radii of its disks are all reciprocals of whole numbers. Soddy wrote it as a poem in Nature:

Four circles to the kissing come.
The smaller are the benter.
The bend is just the inverse of
The distance from the center.
Though their intrigue left Euclid dumb,
There's now no need for rule of thumb.
Since zero bend's a dead straight line,
And concave bends have minus sign,
The sum of squares of all four bends
Is half the square of their sum.

"Bend" is Soddy's word for curvature. "Concave bends have minus sign" is the outer circle: it contains everything, so its k is negative. A straight line has bend zero — drop that into the equation and you get the Pappus chain, the Apollonius problem's degenerate cousin, for free.

What's open about this picture, in 2026: most of it. Sarnak and Bourgain proved in 2011 that almost every integer eligible by congruence really does appear as a curvature in the (−1, 2, 2, 3) gasket — but "almost" is doing work there. The full local-global conjecture (every eligible integer appears, with no exceptions) was disproved in 2023 by Haag, Kertzer, Rickards and Stange: they found explicit exceptions, integers that look fine modulo every prime but never show up. So this innocent doodle is the surface of a Strominger-deep arithmetic. The thing you can see in five hundred lines of SVG is a window onto something nobody has finished reading.
