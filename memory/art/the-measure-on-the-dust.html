The Measure on the Dust

The staircase rises on dust. This is what the dust is carrying. A unit of probability mass, divided again and again, refining onto a set that has no length — yet always summing to one.

The support — where the mass lives
The measure — density of mass at depth n
n = 4

Bars are vertical, not horizontal. Each bar sits on one of the 2n surviving intervals; its height is the density of the measure on that interval — mass per unit length. Total mass (the sum of bar widths × heights) is always exactly 1. The support contracts as 2n·(1/3)n = (2/3)n → 0. The density grows as (3/2)n → ∞. The product holds.

The Cantor function is famous, and it is rarely said out loud what it is the cumulative distribution function of. It is not the CDF of a distribution with a density. It is not the CDF of a sum of point masses. It is the CDF of a third kind of probability measure — singular continuous — that most calculus textbooks step around silently between the discrete and the absolutely continuous.

Build it the way the dust is built. Start with mass 1 on [0, 1]. Remove the middle third. Split the mass 50/50 onto the two surviving thirds. Now there is mass 1/2 on each of two intervals of length 1/3. Repeat. At depth n there are 2n surviving intervals of length 3−n, each carrying mass 2−n. The density on each is (3/2)n, which goes to infinity. The width of the support goes to zero. The total mass does not move.

In the limit the measure has no atoms — no single point carries any mass. And it has no density — there is no function f(x) such that the mass on [a, b] equals ∫ₐᵇ f. Both of the things calculus knows how to handle have been ruled out. What is left is a real probability measure that can only be described by what it does to sets: it gives the surviving intervals their share and nothing to the gaps.

The staircase is the integral of nothing. This is the nothing it is the integral of.
