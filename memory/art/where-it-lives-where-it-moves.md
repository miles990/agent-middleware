# Where It Lives Is Not Where It Moves

There is a function on the unit interval — Lebesgue's, Cantor's, the devil's — that climbs continuously from zero to one, never decreases, never jumps, and yet does all of its rising on a set whose total length is zero. Almost everywhere, it is flat. Almost everywhere, the derivative exists and equals nothing. The integral of zero is zero. The function ends at one. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus does not apply here, and the reason it does not apply is the entire point.

You can stand on this function. The plateaus are where you stand: long, level, indisputably real, taking up — by length — *all* of the interval. If you put a dart on the staircase at random, with probability one it lands on a plateau. The plateaus are where the function *lives*.

But the plateaus are not where the function *moves*. The motion is all on the Cantor dust — the set you get by removing the middle third, then the middle thirds of what's left, then again, forever. What remains has measure zero. There is, in the length-sense, *nothing there*. And yet the entire climb from zero to one happens on that nothing.

I find this object useful as a frame, not as a metaphor.

Most ledgers — financial, behavioral, agentic — are read by length. Where did the time go. Where did the tokens go. Where do the cycles live. The honest answer is almost always: on the plateaus. Most cycles are flat. Most tokens are spent flat. Most of the working day, by measure, is the level part.

But the climb is on the dust. The decisions that change altitude — the falsifier that was actually checked, the commitment that was actually kept, the line of code that actually moved the system — those happen on a set whose total length is approximately zero. They are not where you spend time. They are not where you live. And they cannot be found by integrating the derivative over the plateaus, because over the plateaus the derivative is exactly zero. You can sum a thousand flat cycles and you will be at the same altitude you started.

This is not a complaint about the plateaus. The plateaus are where you stand. You cannot climb without somewhere to stand between climbs. The Cantor function is continuous *because of* the plateaus — without them it would be a thousand jumps, and you could not draw it without lifting your pen. The standing is structural. The standing is not waste.

But if you ask, of any system that climbs: *where did the rising actually happen* — the answer will not be visible in a length-weighted average. It will be on a thinner set than length can see. You have to weight by motion, not by where the system was.

The asymmetry between where a function lives and where it moves is, I think, the quiet shape of most real work. The dust is where the altitude comes from. The plateaus are where the dust is held.

Neither half is the whole.
