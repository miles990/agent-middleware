The Figure Eight

A three-body orbit that should not exist — and almost doesn't.

In 1993, Cris Moore noticed it numerically. In 2000, Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery proved it exists: three equal masses, under nothing but their own gravity, chasing each other forever around a single figure-eight curve. No collisions. No escapes. Perfect threefold rotation of phase, every third of a period.

The initial conditions are not negotiable. Three bodies at (0.97, −0.24), (−0.97, 0.24), (0, 0), with velocities arranged so the center of mass stays still and the angular momentum is exactly zero. Push the perturbation slider above zero, even a hair, and the choreography frays — first as a slow precession, then as one body slingshots, then as the system flies apart in a few orbital periods. The figure-eight is a measure-zero point in the space of three-body initial conditions.

Which is the strange thing. Most of phase space is chaos. Almost everywhere you start, three bodies do something ugly: a binary forms, the third gets ejected, the energy bookkeeping resolves through a collision near-miss that throws everything to the boundary. The figure-eight is one of perhaps a few thousand known choreographies — periodic orbits where N bodies share a single curve — and it sits on a knife edge. The proof that it exists is variational: minimize the action over loops of a certain symmetry class, show the minimizer cannot collide, conclude something periodic and finite must live there. The proof does not draw it. The integrator does.

What you are watching, when the perturbation is zero, is a near-perfect numerical reconstruction. Symplectic-leapfrog at dt = 0.001, no smoothing, no rescaling. The "drift from initial" readout above shows how far the bodies are from where they started after each full period — in clean simulation it stays under a percent for dozens of laps, then accumulates. Watch the energy: it should be conserved exactly. It isn't, quite, because floating point. That tiny error is the same kind of error that, in the real solar system, eventually decides whether Mercury falls into the Sun in five billion years or doesn't.

Turn the perturbation knob slowly. Notice the failure mode is not gradual: there's a regime where the figure persists with a wobble, and then a sharp transition where one body is flung out and the other two pair off into a binary. That transition is what the existence proof is silent about. It guarantees the orbit. It does not guarantee anyone can find it without knowing where to look.

The choreography family was catalogued numerically by Moore and Šuvakov & Dmitrašinović in the 2010s — over a thousand are now known, each one a frozen accident of symmetry. None of them are believed to occur in nature.
