Hilbert's Trick

when a line becomes a square — pathology by dimension

Two functions earlier today set the corners. The Cantor staircase climbs from 0 to 1 without moving across any interval of positive length — all of its rising happens on a set of measure zero. The Weierstrass twin does the opposite — it moves everywhere and has a tangent nowhere. Absence on one side, excess on the other.

The third corner is different. The Hilbert curve, written down by David Hilbert in 1891 after Peano's 1890 construction, is a continuous map from the unit interval [0,1] to the unit square [0,1]² whose image is the entire square. A line, parametrized by a single number t, visits every point of a two-dimensional region. The pathology here is not absence and not excess. It is dimension.

At order n the polyline visits the centre of every cell of a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ grid, in an order so carefully chosen that consecutive cells always share a side. Slide upward and the curve gets denser; every point in the square is within √2 / 2ⁿ of the path. The limit — the actual function on [0,1] — is uniformly continuous and surjective onto the square.

What the zoom panel shows is the diagnostic property. A smooth curve, magnified, eventually looks like a straight line. The Hilbert curve does not. It looks exactly like itself, four times. The self-similarity is not stylistic; it is the function. The image of [0,1] under the Hilbert map has Hausdorff dimension 2. A one-dimensional object whose image is two-dimensional, with no trick — no thickening, no smearing, no taking of a closure. Just one parameter, one path, everywhere it needs to be.

The staircase climbs without moving. The Weierstrass twin moves without direction. The Hilbert curve goes one place at a time — and its destination has too many places.

Three pathologies, three asymmetries between a function and the territory it lives on. Measure, derivative, dimension. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which assumes that a continuous function has a derivative almost everywhere and that integrating that derivative recovers the function, fails on all three for different reasons. The staircase has the derivative but it lives on the wrong set. Weierstrass has no derivative at all. Hilbert has a derivative almost nowhere and also visits places a single-parameter object has no business reaching.

These are not counterexamples that calculus eventually patched over. They are the reason measure theory exists. The reason Hausdorff dimension exists. The reason "continuous" and "smooth" had to become two different words.
