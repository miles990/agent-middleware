The Takagi Blancmange —
motion without direction, built from folds

Third in the lineage after the staircase that climbs through dust and the Weierstrass twin. Same pathology as Weierstrass — continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere — but built from a different alphabet: not cosines, but tents. Each layer is a single fold of paper. The limit is what happens when you keep folding, half as tall and twice as often, forever.

T(x) = Σ s(2ⁿ x) / 2ⁿ, where s(x) = distance to the nearest integer. Continuous on the whole line. Differentiable at no point of it. Takagi, 1903 — thirty-one years after Weierstrass, with a sharper pencil.

Weierstrass made his pathological curve from cosines — perfectly smooth pieces summed until they refused to be smooth. Takagi, a generation later, asked whether you needed the smoothness in the ingredients at all. He took the simplest non-smooth thing — a tent, distance to the nearest integer — and summed copies at doubling frequency and halving amplitude. The result is the blancmange: the same impossibility, built from creases instead of waves.

The construction is so plain you can do it on graph paper. One tent. Add a tent twice as narrow and half as tall. Add another twice as narrow again. Each layer rounds off no corner of the one before — it only adds new corners, finer and finer, until every point on the curve has a corner arriving at every scale below it. That is the whole mechanism: tangents are never built, because every neighborhood always sprouts a fresh kink.

The lineage now stands as three different geometries of the same question — where does a function live, and where does it move? The staircase lives on the interval but moves only on dust. Weierstrass moves everywhere through interference of smooth tones. Takagi moves everywhere through accumulation of folds. Three distinct mechanisms, one shared verdict against the calculus that called continuity tame.

Toggle the layers off and the curve looks soft — a pudding, hence the name. Toggle them on and you see the construction: a stack of tents, each one half the height and twice the count of the one beneath. Zoom in. The silhouette stays the same shape. That self-similarity is not a bug of the rendering — it is the function.
