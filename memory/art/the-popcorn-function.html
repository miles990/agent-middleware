The Popcorn Function

A spike at every rational, silence at every irrational, height that remembers the denominator.
rational p/q, spike height 1/q irrational baseline f(x)=0

Carl Johannes Thomae wrote it down in 1875. For any x in [0,1] reduce it to lowest terms p/q if you can; assign 1/q. If you cannot — if x is irrational — assign zero. The resulting function looks like a forest of vertical spikes hung from a flat floor: taller spikes at small denominators, shorter ones crowding into the gaps, an infinite descent of crowding toward every neighborhood.

Here is the surprise. The function is continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every rational. Near any irrational point, only finitely many rationals have denominator ≤ q, so almost all neighbors are short spikes and irrational zeros — the function is squeezed to zero in the limit. Near any rational p/q, no matter how small a window you pick, the spike at p/q sits at height 1/q while neighbors come arbitrarily close to zero. The break can never be closed.

The Cantor staircase moves only on a measure-zero set. The popcorn function breaks only on a measure-zero set. One is continuity violated by an absent derivative; the other is continuity preserved by an absent denominator. They share a floorplan and disagree about which room is the kitchen.

What the picture shows: a slider over qmax reveals more spikes — but each new one is shorter than its predecessors at the same scale. The denser the rationals visible, the closer the silhouette presses to the baseline. Push qmax high enough and you can see the limit: the irrationals win, the function flattens, the staircase of arithmetic dissolves into the measure-zero noise it always was.

The popcorn function is not pathology. It is the calmest thing in the lineage. Every other piece here — Weierstrass, Cantor, Brownian, Conway's base-13 — refuses some classical expectation. Popcorn obeys: it is continuous on a set of full measure, and where it isn't, it apologizes by being very short. It is the function that tells the truth about how dense the rationals actually are.

Stand on an irrational point and the spikes melt around you. Stand on a rational and you are the only thing standing.
