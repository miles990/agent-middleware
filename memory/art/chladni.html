Chladni's Plate

a square plate vibrates; the sand walks off the loud parts and sits on the silent ones

Ernst Chladni, 1787: bow a metal plate at the edge, sprinkle sand on top, and the grains skitter away from every place that moves. They settle on the curves that don't. Napoleon, watching this in 1809, put up a 3000-franc prize for whoever could derive the curves from first principles. Sophie Germain took it, eventually, in 1816 — the third attempt — with a fourth-order plate equation that nobody else had the courage to write down.

f(x,y) = cos(mπx) cos(nπy) − cos(nπx) cos(mπy)
3 2

The plate has natural modes — standing waves that fit its shape and edge conditions. For a square plate with free edges, two pure cosine modes (m,n) and (n,m) share the same frequency, so the plate vibrates in their combination. Subtract one from the other and you get a pattern that flips sign across diagonals; add them and you get one that doesn't. Sand finds the zeros either way.

The function above is the cheap version. The honest plate equation — Germain's, refined by Kirchhoff — is ∇⁴w + (ρh/D) ∂²w/∂t² = 0, fourth-order in space, and on anything that isn't a rectangle the eigenmodes have no closed form. Round plates need Bessel functions. Triangular, irregular, or hole-pierced plates need a computer. Chladni had no computer; he had a violin bow and a steady hand, and he catalogued a hundred patterns before any of them had a name.

Drag the sliders. m=n gives a checkerboard; coprime (m,n) gives the busy lacework Chladni's plates are famous for; large (m,n) tells you why he needed the bow and not a thumbnail.

Render: per-pixel evaluation of f(x,y) over a 320×320 grid; darkness ∝ √|f| so the nodal lines (where the sand settles) come up black. No libraries, no shaders, no audio — the plate is silent here, which is the only honest part of the simulation.
