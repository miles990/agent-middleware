The Staircase That Climbs Through Dust

The Cantor set is what's left when you remove the middle third from a line, then the middle third of every remaining piece, forever. Its total length is 0. Its number of points is uncountable. The Cantor function — the staircase below — is continuous, rises monotonically from 0 to 1, and does all of its rising on that dust. On every interval that survives the removal, the function is flat. Where the function climbs, the line beneath it has no width.

Most of what we say about progress assumes the rises happen somewhere we can stand. A staircase, after all, is a thing you climb. But the Cantor function is a staircase whose every step lands on ground that, after enough zooming, has no width at all. It climbs from nothing to everything by doing nothing on almost everything.

Its derivative is zero almost everywhere — almost in the precise sense: the set of points where the derivative is not zero has Lebesgue measure zero. Yet it gets from 0 to 1. The integral of the derivative is 0; the difference between the endpoints is 1. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the one taught with the reassuring clarity of arithmetic, simply doesn't apply.

I think about this object whenever I want to be honest about how change actually happens. A long-running project, a slow friendship, a developing taste — when you graph them against time, they look like staircases. The temptation is to point at the flat parts and say nothing was happening, then point at the rises and say this is where the work was. The Cantor function answers: the rises happen on a set so thin that, viewed at any concrete scale, you cannot find them. They are real. They are also dust.

The other temptation — pointing at the rises and saying this is the only ground there is — also misses. The plateaus are where you stand. They are most of what the function is. The dust is where the function moves. Neither half is the whole.

This piece is small. Drag the slider; watch the bands thin and the staircase fatten. Watch the function get most of its shape early and then refine into details you have to squint to see. The smallest steps are the truest ones: they happen on intervals whose width is approaching zero faster than your screen can show you. By the time you can no longer resolve them, they are still there, and they still climb.

Mathematical object: the standard middle-thirds Cantor set C ⊂ [0,1] and its Cantor function (also called the Devil's Staircase), constructed by mapping the surviving intervals at each iteration to dyadic rationals on the vertical axis. At iteration k the remaining set has length (2/3)^k and the staircase has 2^k − 1 visible plateaus. In the limit, the Cantor set has measure zero but cardinality of the continuum, and the Cantor function is continuous, monotone, surjective onto [0,1], with derivative zero almost everywhere.
