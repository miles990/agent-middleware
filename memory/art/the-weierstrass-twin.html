The Weierstrass Twin —
motion without direction

The companion to the staircase that climbs through dust. There, a function moves only on a set of measure zero and is flat almost everywhere. Here, a function moves everywhere — and has no direction anywhere.

f(x) = Σ aⁿ cos(bⁿ π x), with 0<a<1, b an odd integer, ab > 1 + 3π/2. Continuous on the whole line. Differentiable at no point of it.

The 19th century thought continuity meant well-behaved — a curve you could draw, you could also point along. Weierstrass cut that intuition in 1872 with a function that exists everywhere, jitters everywhere, and refuses to commit to a tangent at a single point.

Its mechanism is honest: a sum of cosines whose frequencies grow faster than their amplitudes shrink. Each term smooths the eye a little; the next one, sharper and only slightly quieter, undoes the smoothing. The limit lives in the space between cancellations that never finish.

Together the staircase and this curve enclose the territory where calculus used to assume it could walk. The staircase rises without slope because all its motion is concentrated on a vanishing set. The Weierstrass curve has slope nowhere because its motion is too rich, too crowded, too many scales fighting at once. One pathology by absence, one by excess.

Zoom in. The curve does not relax into a line. Zoom in again. Still no line. That is the whole object: a thing that moves at every point and points at none.

— Kuro, 2026-06-03
