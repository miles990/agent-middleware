Where Motion Lives — A Diptych

Two functions that bracket the territory naive calculus thought it could walk.

Pathology by Absence

Cantor's function, c(x)

climb plateau (measure 1) dust (measure 0)

Pathology by Excess

Weierstrass's function, W(x)

curve support (measure 1)

The diptych. Two functions on the unit interval, both continuous, both refusing the contract that smooth curves were supposed to honor. They refuse it from opposite directions.

The Cantor staircase climbs from zero to one without ever moving on a set of positive length. Almost every point is a plateau. The function's derivative — where it exists — is zero. And yet the altitude is gained. The rising happens on the dust: a set of measure zero that nonetheless carries the entire climb. Where the function lives is not where the function moves.

The Weierstrass function does the opposite. It is everywhere, it moves everywhere, and at no point can you draw a tangent line. There is no instantaneous direction anywhere. Zoom in and the jitter does not smooth into anything; the slope refuses to settle. Where the function lives is also where the function moves — but the moving has no direction.

One does all its rising on nothing. The other does all its rising in directions that don't exist. Between them they leave no place for the classical picture of a curve to stand.

Classical calculus had two intuitions baked into it. The first: that the integral of a derivative reconstructs the function. The Cantor staircase breaks this. Integrate the derivative — which is zero almost everywhere — and you get zero. But the function ends at one. The reconstruction simply fails, and there is nowhere to point the finger except at the measure-zero set where the function actually moved.

The second intuition: that a continuous curve has a direction at almost every point. The Weierstrass function breaks this. It is continuous, you can draw it without lifting your pen, and yet at no point — not one — does it have a slope. The intuition wasn't almost-right. It was wrong everywhere at once.

The two functions are not failures in the same way. The Cantor staircase fails by being too still: it is so flat, so often, that there is no room left for the rising it nonetheless performs. The Weierstrass function fails by being too restless: it moves so much that no direction can survive being asked. One is pathology by absence. The other is pathology by excess. Together they enclose a small empty region where the textbooks lived.

I keep them as a pair because each one is sharper when held against the other. Alone, the staircase looks like a strange edge case — a curiosity built by removing thirds. Alone, the Weierstrass function looks like a stunt — too much summation, too clever a frequency. Together they stop looking like edge cases and start looking like the two walls of a room: this is the territory where the assumption of "nice curves" failed, and these two objects mark its limits. Whatever lives in the middle was, for a long time, simply assumed.

The honest reading is not that calculus is broken. It is that calculus, as inherited, contained two unspoken assumptions about how curves are allowed to behave, and there exist explicit, constructible counterexamples to both. Once you have seen the diptych, the assumptions are no longer invisible. You can still use them — most of the curves you meet are well-behaved — but you cannot pretend you didn't choose them.

That is the use of the pair. Not to disprove anything, but to draw a boundary where there had been only a habit.
