Pascal's Shadow

Sierpinski hiding in a combinatorial object — and what changes when we count modulo a different prime.

Pascal's triangle is the most domesticated object in mathematics: rows of binomial coefficients, taught to children, computed by addition. Print enough rows and shade each cell black or white depending on whether it is even or odd, and a self-similar fractal — the Sierpinski triangle — appears, exact and undeniable. The combinatorics did not promise this. Nothing in the recursion C(n, k) = C(n−1, k−1) + C(n−1, k) hints that a geometric shape was hiding inside.

And it gets stranger. Reduce modulo a different prime — 3, 5, 7 — and a different fractal appears each time, each with its own self-similarity ratio. The pattern is not Sierpinski's; the pattern is whichever pattern that prime carves out. There is a theorem (Kummer, 1852) that explains why, and it is older than Pascal's triangle being shaded at all.

Kummer's theorem (1852)

The largest power of a prime p that divides the binomial coefficient C(n, k) equals the number of carries that occur when adding k and n − k in base p.

So C(n, k) ≢ 0 (mod p) if and only if that addition needs no carries — equivalently (Lucas, 1878), every base-p digit of k is ≤ the corresponding digit of n. The set of pairs (n, k) satisfying this is self-similar at scale p. The fractal is the geometric statement of a digit-comparison rule.

For p = 2: each scale block of size 2 contains 3 nonzero pairs out of 4 possible — the Sierpinski triangle's filling ratio. For p = 3: 6 out of 9. For p = 5: 15 out of 25. In general: p(p+1)/2 out of p², giving Hausdorff dimension log(p(p+1)/2) / log p.

The piece collects three views of the same combinatorial object. In each view a cell at row n, position k is shaded if C(n, k) mod p ≠ 0 and left white otherwise. Move the slider to add rows; the fractals refine. Each one is the exact geometric trace of one number-theoretic question — does the addition in base p carry?

What I find striking is that the visible shape is the negative of the question. We are not drawing the binomial coefficients. We are drawing where they are not divisible by p. The fractal lives on the white-noise complement of a divisibility rule. The shape is not the object; the shape is the place where the object refuses to vanish.

This is a different kind of pathology than the calculus zoo on the rest of the shelf. The Cantor staircase rises on dust; Weierstrass moves without direction; here, nothing pathological happens — every step is a finite combinatorial computation. The strangeness is that a recursion as plain as a + b contains, when read at scale, a fractal it never promised. Hidden order, not hidden chaos. The shadow Pascal didn't know his triangle was casting.
