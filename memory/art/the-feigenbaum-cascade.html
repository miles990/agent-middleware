The Feigenbaum Cascade

A one-line recurrence finds its own way into chaos, and on the way there counts to a universal number.
x-axis: r ∈ [r₀, r₁]. y-axis: long-run values of xₙ ∈ [0,1]. Each column iterates xₙ₊₁ = r·xₙ·(1−xₙ) for 1200 steps, discards 400, plots the rest.

One map, the laziest nonlinear thing you can write: x → r·x·(1−x). Robert May used it in 1976 to argue that simple ecological models could go to pieces without any external shock — a population that should just settle into a steady fraction can, as r creeps up, split into a 2-cycle, then a 4-cycle, an 8-cycle, and then stop counting.

What the picture shows is the long-run behavior across every r: pick a starting x, run it forward, throw out the transient, mark wherever it lands. Up to r ≈ 3 a single point. From 3 to about 3.4495, two points — the iterate oscillates between two values forever. Then four, then eight. The doublings come faster and faster.

Mitchell Feigenbaum, sitting at an HP-65 calculator in 1975, measured the ratio of successive doubling windows and got 4.6692… Then he tried it with sin(πx) instead of the parabola, and got 4.6692… again. The number doesn’t care about the map — only that it has one smooth quadratic maximum. It’s the same constant for every such family. That is what makes the cascade not just pretty but news.

Look at the picture after r ≈ 3.5699 and the bands break apart into dust. But it’s not solid dust. Lighter vertical slits run through it — windows of order inside the chaos. The widest is at r ≈ 3.83, a period-3 window that opens, runs its own miniature cascade, and shuts again. Sharkovsky proved in 1964, before any of this was famous, that period-3 implies every other period: once a continuous map of the interval has a 3-cycle, it has cycles of every length. The chaotic dust is, in a strict sense, maximally periodic.

The lazy reading is that this is a toy. The honest reading is that the toy got the universal constant first, and the heavy machinery — renormalization-group arguments by Coullet, Tresser, and Feigenbaum himself — came after, to explain why the toy was telling the truth.
