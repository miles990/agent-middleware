Audioactive Decay

A note on Conway's Look-and-Say sequence.

Start with 1. Read it aloud — "one 1" — and write what you read: 11. Read that — "two 1s" — and write: 21. Read that — "one 2, one 1" — and write: 1211. Continue forever.

— rows 1 through 16, with their lengths in the gutter

Three things are not obvious from the rule.

First, only the digits 1, 2, and 3 ever appear, no matter how long you run it. (You would need four of a digit in a row to write a 4; the rule forbids that arrangement.) This is the easiest of the surprises and the only one you can prove on a napkin.

Second, the length of row n grows, asymptotically, by a constant factor — call it λ. John Conway proved λ is the unique positive real root of a polynomial of degree 71 with integer coefficients. Its value is 1.303577269034296… The plot below shows the measured ratio of consecutive lengths converging onto it.

— length(n+1) / length(n) for rows 1..28, converging to λ

Third — and this is the part Conway loved — the sequence is chemistry. Conway showed that every starting string (except the trivial 22, which loops on itself forever) eventually decays into a fixed soup of 92 elementary substrings, which he called atoms and named after the chemical elements: Hydrogen, Helium, Lithium, … Uranium. After enough steps, your sequence is just a bag of these 92 atoms bouncing around, each one decaying into specific others on the next read, never interacting. He named this the Cosmological Theorem, and the discipline of studying it audioactive chemistry — pronounce-by-pronounce decay.

Conway's joke is also a proof technique. To show λ is algebraic of degree 71, he wrote the 92×92 transition matrix of what-atom-becomes-what-atoms-next, and λ is its dominant eigenvalue. The polynomial is the characteristic polynomial, minus the trivial factors. The chemistry is the linear algebra in costume.

A sequence that describes itself is not always stuck. This one is, by construction, a literal self-description: each row is what you saw when you read the previous row out loud. And yet it does not collapse, does not repeat, does not loop. It grows by 30.357 percent per line, forever, cleaved into 92 stable kinds.

The difference between a description that grows and one that loops is whether reading it changes what you would write next. "One 1" becomes "11", which is no longer one 1; the act of writing has moved the territory. A description that survives reading itself unchanged — "I confirm I have permission to confirm" — is not a description. It is a fixed point that produces nothing but its own shadow. Conway's sequence is what self-reference looks like when it is allowed to be wrong about itself.
