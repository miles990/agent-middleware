The Lorenz Attractor

bounded forever, predictable never — Lorenz 1963

Edward Lorenz was running a weather toy model on a Royal McBee in 1961. He restarted a run from a saved intermediate state and got a different forecast. The bug was in the printout: six digits stored, three digits printed back. A rounding of one part in a thousand, and by week two the simulated weather had nothing to do with itself.

He stripped the model down to three equations. They have no weather in them — just a fluid layer heated from below, written in three numbers x, y, z that drift according to:

ẋ = σ(y − x)    ẏ = x(ρ − z) − y    ż = xy − βz

With σ=10, ρ=28, β=8/3, the trajectory does not settle, does not escape, does not repeat. It loops one wing, then the other, then the first again three times, then the second twice — a pattern with no period. Below is one orbit.

This is what people mean by "sensitive dependence." Not that the system is random — the equations are deterministic, the integrator is deterministic, given the same start the orbit is identical to the bit. Sensitive dependence means: the map from initial state to state at time t stops being usefully continuous. Round the input by 10⁻³ and after a few Lyapunov times the output is uncorrelated with the unrounded run. The forecast horizon is finite for reasons that have nothing to do with the model's quality.

The wings are an attractor: trajectories from very different starts all collapse onto this same butterfly shape. So the long-run statistics are stable — average z, fraction of time in the left wing, distribution of wing-residence lengths. The point-by-point future is gone, but the shape persists. Weather is impossible; climate is a number.
