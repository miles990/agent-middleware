Penrose Tiling

a fifty-year detour to the hat

Pick a rhomb at the centre. Walk a metre in any direction. Whatever patch of tiles you land on appears, exactly, somewhere else — infinitely often, in every direction. But the whole tiling never returns to itself. Slide it any nonzero amount and it no longer matches. Aperiodic, but saturated with local repetition. The geometry hides its lack of period inside its surplus of pattern.

Roger Penrose published these two shapes in 1974. There were earlier aperiodic sets — Robert Berger's 1966 set of 20,426 tiles, then his own collapse to 104, then Robinson's 6, then Penrose's 6, then 4. Two was the floor he reached. Could one shape do it? A single tile that forces aperiodicity, with no helper? The question hung for forty-nine years.

The picture above is generated by deflation. Start with ten fat triangle halves arranged around a point. Each subdivision step replaces every triangle with smaller triangles, scaled by 1/φ. Repeat. Pair the triangles back into rhombs to draw. The construction is self-similar — every patch you see at depth n is a shrunken version of a patch that will reappear at depth n+k, exactly, somewhere else. That is what "no period" feels like up close: a system where every neighbourhood is a echo of some past or future neighbourhood, but never of itself shifted.

Penrose tilings forced a category that crystallography did not have a word for. Crystals had long-range periodic order. Glass had no order. These had long-range aperiodic order — sharp diffraction peaks without translation symmetry — and they were considered mathematical toys.

Then in 1982, Dan Shechtman aimed an electron beam at a rapidly cooled aluminium-manganese alloy and got tenfold diffraction symmetry — forbidden in any periodic crystal. He was told to leave the group. Linus Pauling, until his death, called him a "quasi-scientist." In 2011 Shechtman received the Nobel Prize alone.

"There can be no such creatures as quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists." — Linus Pauling, c. 1988

By then the toy was a material. Aperiodic order had become a classification of matter.

In November 2022 an amateur in Yorkshire named David Smith, playing with paper polykites, noticed that one particular thirteen-sided shape — eight kites glued together — seemed to tile only in non-repeating ways. He emailed Craig Kaplan. By March 2023, Smith, Kaplan, Joseph Myers and Chaim Goodman-Strauss had a proof (2303.10798). The "hat". A single tile, with reflections allowed, that forces aperiodicity. The fifty-year question, closed.

Then critics asked: does it really count if you need the mirror image? Two months later the same team posted the "spectre" (2305.17743), a tile that tiles aperiodically using no reflections — a chiral monotile. The floor of "how few shapes do you need" finally hit one.

The drawing above is still the rhombs, because the rhombs are honest: the deflation rule fits in fifteen lines of JavaScript, and their beauty is intelligible from a glance. The hat's substitution rules need four metatile types and several pages of careful diagram-matching to verify; rendering one is an essay, not a sketch.

What is small enough to draw is rarely what is hardest to find.
