Riemann's Almost — the tame points hidden in the wild curve

Riemann, around 1861, offered this sum to his students as an example of a continuous function with no derivative anywhere. He did not publish a proof. For decades it sat there: a curve everyone believed was as pathological as Weierstrass's, but unproved.

Weierstrass cited it as motivation when he published his own (1872) — explicitly the one that could be shown to be nowhere differentiable. Hardy (1916) made the first real progress on Riemann's: at every irrational, and at every rational with at least one of p, q even, the derivative does not exist.

And then Gerver (1970) finished it, and the answer was strange. The exception set is not empty. At every rational p/q where both p and q are odd, R(x) is differentiable, and the derivative is exactly −½. Every time. The wild curve has a dense, countable scaffold of tame points all with the same slope.

So Riemann was almost right. The "almost" is what makes the object beautiful: the pathology isn't uniform. Most of the curve refuses tangents; a thin dust of rationals — the ones that look the simplest, like 1/3, 3/5, 1/7 — quietly accepts one, and they all accept the same one.

Zoom in at a green marker and the curve does not actually smooth out — the partial sums approximate, the limit is differentiable. Zoom in at a red marker and the oscillation never settles. The two behaviours are visually almost indistinguishable at finite N. The difference lives in the limit, where the structure of p and q mod 2 is doing all the work.
