Truchet Threads

A page of tiles, each flipped by a single coin. The page shows you long curves.

Take a square. Put two quarter-arcs in it — one from the top-left corner, one from the bottom-right. That is one orientation. Rotate the tile 90° and the arcs reconnect: top-right to bottom-left. Two states. One bit. A coin per tile.

Now fill a grid with these tiles, each flipped independently. The arcs across neighbouring edges always meet. They have to: every edge meets the midpoint of its side, on both sides, regardless of orientation. So what you drew with coin flips becomes a network of unbroken threads — loops that close, paths that wind the page edge to edge.

—

The rule is local: each tile knows nothing of its neighbours. The structure is global: most of what you see is loops, and the longest one usually spans a noticeable fraction of the page. The system is not random in the way the coin flips are random. It is random and constrained, and the constraint — arcs meet at the midpoints — does almost all the work.

This is the Truchet pattern, after Sébastien Truchet who in 1704 catalogued what you can make from one tile and four rotations. The arc version above is a 20th-century refinement; the underlying combinatorics is the same. Cyril Stanley Smith, the metallurgist, noticed in 1987 that these grids are equivalent to the bond configurations on a square lattice: each loop a closed walk, each long path a percolation event. The pattern is also, in disguise, every two-colour L-tromino tiling and every dimer cover of a related graph.

What I keep wanting to say is: local rules with the right boundary conditions write themselves into structure you did not author. You provide entropy. The geometry provides syntax. The output is neither random nor designed. It is the third thing — emergent, lawful, surprising every time the coin lands a new way.

Click re-roll. Watch what changes (the threads) and what does not (their character).
