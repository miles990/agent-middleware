The Fat Cantor Set

— two dusts side by side. one weighs zero. one weighs half.

The construction is the same gesture twice with one parameter changed. From the unit interval, remove the open middle third. From each of the two survivors, remove the open middle third again. Repeat. What is left is the Cantor set — uncountable, perfect, totally disconnected, and of Lebesgue measure zero. Calculus textbooks pause on it for a moment and then file it under negligible.

Now repeat the same gesture, but at step n remove from each survivor an open middle interval of length 4⁻ⁿ instead of one third. At step 1 you remove 1/4. At step 2 you remove two pieces of length 1/16, total 1/8. At step 3, four pieces of length 1/64, total 1/16. The sum of all removals is the geometric series 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + … = 1/2. What remains — the Smith–Volterra–Cantor set — looks identical in temperament to its thin cousin. It is uncountable, perfect, totally disconnected. It contains no interval. Nowhere on the line is it dense. And yet it has Lebesgue measure exactly 1/2.

The two pictures above are aligned on the same axis. Push the depth slider and watch them lose mass together. The top set keeps losing more aggressively at each step until, in the limit, only dust remains and the dust has no length. The bottom set loses less and less; by the limit, half the line has been removed and half — distributed among uncountably many points, none of them touching any neighbourhood — is still there. You cannot point to any interval the bottom set contains. You cannot walk along it. But if you sweep a ruler over the unit interval and ask how much of this is fat-Cantor, the answer is half the ruler.

The thin Cantor set lets us believe a comforting equation: nowhere-dense = small = measure zero. The fat Cantor set is what the equation forgot. Topological smallness and measure-theoretic smallness are two different small. The line is wider than the words for thinness can fit.
